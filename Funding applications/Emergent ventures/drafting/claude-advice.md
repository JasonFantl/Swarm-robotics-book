# Winning an Emergent Ventures Grant: A Strategy Report for a Visual, Interactive Swarm Robotics Textbook

## TL;DR
- Emergent Ventures (EV) funds *people*, not just projects: Tyler Cowen reviews a short (max 1,500-word) application himself, deliberately ignores credentials, and bets on ambitious, energetic, curious "emergent" talent with "zero to one" moonshot ideas. Your revealed profile (6-year blog, HN front page, blog adopted as course material, Raytheon BBN/NASA/Anduril track record, AI safety fellowship, PhD plans) is an unusually strong fit and should be foregrounded as evidence of demonstrated follow-through.
- Educational and explanatory content is squarely in EV's wheelhouse: it has repeatedly funded economics videos, science-communication YouTube, Substacks, online courses, physics/math instruction, and progress-oriented writing. A visual, interactive, open textbook for undergraduates that also equips professors is a credible EV project, but you must make it feel *feasible* (concrete plan, milestones) and *scalable* (a durable resource with a route to self-sufficiency), because vague ambition is the most common reason applications stall.
- Ask for a specific number with 2-3 line items (EV grants typically run from a few hundred dollars to roughly $50,000; the median has historically been in the $10,000–$25,000 range, and "tens of thousands, not millions" is the norm), expect a fast decision (Mercatus says responses are "typically sent within a week," and TIME notes applicants "get a response in a week or two") and possibly a short unstructured video call, be ready for a revenue/sustainability question, and treat the "consensus view you agree with" prompt as a sincerity test, not an invitation to be contrarian.

## Key Findings

**1. What EV is.** Emergent Ventures is a low-overhead fellowship and grant program at the Mercatus Center, George Mason University, launched in 2018 with an initial $1 million grant from the Thiel Foundation (Cowen has said the program has raised "more than $4.25 million," including that $1 million Thiel gift, and it is also backed by donors such as Schmidt Futures). Tyler Cowen administers it personally. Its stated mission is to "jumpstart high-reward ideas—moonshots in many cases—that advance prosperity, opportunity, liberty, and well-being," welcoming "the unusual and the unorthodox." Per the Mercatus Center and TIME (June 2025), "Since its launch, Emergent Ventures has funded more than 1,000 individuals around the world," and Cowen was named to TIME's inaugural 2025 list of the 100 most influential people in philanthropy (in the "innovators" category) for the program. Cowen told TIME, "I've focused on trying to mobilize talent that otherwise is not discovered or inspired," adding that he "screens most of the applications himself."

**2. The selection philosophy is talent-first and credential-blind.** The official application explicitly says: "We probably don't care much about your formal education, credentials, or awards, unless they're particularly germane to who you are or your idea... credentials are not what will impress us." Cowen has said the grant goes "to people we perceive as important talents, either now or in the future." In interviews he says he looks for "energy level, persistence, curiosity and some level of caring or authenticity," and stresses that "how good the proposal is is really very important" — the writing itself is a primary signal.

**3. The mechanics are deliberately minimal and fast.** One online form; a proposal of no more than 1,500 words that "must stand on its own"; optional supplementary materials and a multimedia URL; a one-tweet summary; and a ballpark budget. There is no CV requirement, no letters of reference, no test scores. Responses are "typically sent within a week." Grants range widely (roughly $1,000 to $50,000, occasionally more; many are small "travel grants"). Reporting is famously light: one grantee describes paperwork taking under a week, money wired within another week, and the only obligation being a one-page report a year later.

**4. Educational and explanatory-content projects are a well-established funding category.** EV has funded a large number of content/education projects, which strongly de-risks the *category* of your pitch (see Details for named examples).

**5. The most common failure mode is ambition without feasibility.** In his Mercatus essay "A Behind-the-Scenes Look at Emergent Ventures so Far," Cowen writes: "Marginal applications... may be ambitious and interesting, but lack sufficient detail to convince me that they are feasible. Or they may seem rather smart, but fall into areas where there has already been plenty of capital thrown around, or are otherwise a bit too crowded."

## Details

### What Emergent Ventures is looking for (from Cowen's own words and writing)

EV grew out of Cowen's critique of mainstream philanthropy. In his November 2018 essay "The philosophy and practicality of Emergent Ventures," he argues that big foundations have "too many vetoes," produce "relatively conservative, consensus-oriented proposals," and carry "high fixed costs" that discriminate against very small grants. His answer is the "pop-up foundation" run by a single evaluator who "can take risks with the proposals, unencumbered by the need to cover fixed costs." This is why EV can and does make tiny grants, and why unusual/risky bets are a feature rather than a bug.

Tony Kulesa's widely cited essay "Tyler Cowen is the best curator of talent in the world" (endorsed by Cowen on Marginal Revolution as "quite accurate and insightful") identifies four components:
- **Distribution:** EV stays "a bit weird and obscure" so the applicant pool is high-quality and "earnest." Cowen: "I try to stay a bit weird and obscure enough that mostly quite smart people are writing me" (Marginal Revolution has roughly 1 million monthly readers per SimilarWeb, per Kulesa).
- **Application:** "laser focused on the quality of the applicant's ideas," boiling out "the noise of credentials, references, and test scores."
- **Selection:** Cowen's decades of "cracking cultural codes" — trained taste.
- **Inspiration:** Cowen pushes winners to be more ambitious; the grant is "a push to be more ambitious," and the money makes the vision "more vivid or more focal."

From his book *Talent* (with Daniel Gross) and related interviews, the signals he prizes: **ambition** ("How ambitious are you?" is his favorite question for potential leaders — one he calls "surprisingly difficult to fake"), **energy/stamina** (he prefers stamina to grit), **curiosity** (the "open browser tabs" question tests demonstrated, spontaneous interests), and **persistence/authenticity**. He is skeptical of pure credentialism and IQ ("intelligence is usually overrated"), and he has said he is especially high on 14-to-19-year-olds. Weird, underrated, and geographically or socially overlooked talent is the explicit target.

### The application mechanics in detail

The single application form (mercatus.tfaforms.net) asks for:
- Basic personal information, location, Twitter handle (optional), an "Affected Region" drop-down (select "USA"/"No specified area" for a general project; India, Africa & the Caribbean, Ukraine, and Taiwan have dedicated verticals with their own leads — Shruti Rajagopalan for India, Rasheed Griffith for Africa & the Caribbean).
- "How do you describe your idea in a tweet?" (a one-line hook).
- The proposal (≤1,500 words), structured in three parts (below).
- Estimated budget and a breakdown of expenses.
- Optional file uploads (note: **no PDFs accepted** — use doc/docx, images, slides) and one multimedia URL.

**The three proposal prompts (verbatim):**
1. "The first part of the proposal should be about you. Tell us your personal story, and how it relates to what you wish to do... credentials are not what will impress us."
2. "Second, what is one mainstream or 'consensus' view that you absolutely agree with? (This is our version of a 'trick' question, reversing the now-fashionable contrarianism.)"
3. "The third part should be about your idea. Convince us that this is a great idea worth investing in, and tell us what is new or unusual in your vision and understanding. What's the problem you intend to solve?"

Plus: "If you have a ballpark budget (with revenue sources and expenses), let us know the bare basics now; we won't hold you to it strictly. There is NO typical grant range or average award amount – funding is project specific." And: tell them how long you've worked on it, full- vs part-time, and any partners/supporters.

**Grant size and timeline.** Officially there is "no typical grant range." In practice, the Chronicle of Philanthropy's profile of Cowen reports: "Currently the median grant is in the $10,000 to $25,000 range. Cowen says the support is likely to increase for grantees that show progress" (the profile cites one $10,000 grant as an example). Third-party trackers and grantee accounts converge on a broader $1,000–$50,000 range, with many small travel grants of a few hundred to a few thousand dollars. Decisions are fast — Mercatus states "responses are typically sent within a week," and TIME notes applicants "get a response in a week or two." There is no fixed deadline; applications are rolling, and applicants are encouraged to apply quickly because "funds are limited." Winners are announced in numbered cohort posts on Marginal Revolution (the program is past its 55th cohort as of mid-2026), each listing names with a one-line project description. Some grantees are invited to a short, unstructured video call with Cowen; some receive an in-residence fellowship visit to George Mason.

**Travel grant vs project grant.** EV routinely makes small "travel grants" (e.g., to visit a research hub, attend a conference, or relocate temporarily) distinct from larger project grants; "general career support" is also an explicit, common category. This matters tactically: you can request a modest, well-defined sum rather than a large project budget, and modest asks are easy for a single evaluator to approve. (For context on scale at the extreme end, EV's Fast Grants spin-off "raised more than $50 million and awarded 260 grants" for COVID-19 research, with grants of "$10,000 to $500,000" decided "within 14 days" — a separate program, but illustrative of the low-overhead, fast-decision DNA.)

### Analysis of successful applications and grantees

**Education/content is a proven category.** Documented EV grantees in adjacent territory include: Aras Zirgulis (Vilnius, "short economics videos"); Justin Kuiper (Bay Area, "Progress Studies ideas for video"); Scott Ellis (science education, "biographies of scientists on YouTube"); Aliaksandra Melnichenka ("to support science and math writing"); Mariia Marinichenko ("physics and math instruction in Ukrainian"); Kyla Scanlon (economics content and a documentary); David Perell (podcasting/writing, first cohort); Sam Atis (independent writing/Substack); Lydia Laurenson (Substack writing, cohort 43); Cedric Warny (an online "Governance Studies" course); and Krishiv Thakuria (Aceflow, AI learning tools, a self-reported $7,000). Robotics and drones appear frequently too: David Yang (14, Vancouver, robotics), Dima Yanovsky (MIT, robotics), Sri Anumakonda (robotic hands), plus multiple drone/satellite-robotics grantees. So both halves of your project — technical robotics content and educational exposition — map onto EV's revealed preferences.

**Grantee advice (from people who actually got grants):**

*Cedric Warny* published his full application. His structure: an "About me" that leads with **concrete, unusual, self-directed accomplishments** (he wrote a tax code from scratch for Belgium and made a viral video about it) rather than credentials; a **data-backed consensus view** ("investment in education has some of the most positive externalities of any investment"; "rule of law is by far the biggest source of wealth in the world"), each supported with specific citations and tied to his project; and an idea framed as a scalable "zero-to-one" analog to a respected thing ("Progress Studies, but for governance"). Critically, his interview with Cowen "quickly focused on revenue self-sufficiency as the main sticking point" — Cowen asked him to come back with a revenue model. **Lesson: have a sustainability answer ready even for a seemingly non-commercial project.**

*Lydia Laurenson* reached her grant through genuine intellectual engagement — she emailed Cowen substantive thoughts on one of his blog posts, and he replied that he was a fan of her work. Her initial ask (a print magazine) was rejected — "Tyler responded saying that he is bearish on print magazines" — and he repeatedly steered her toward Substack. She reframed and got funded. **Lesson: if Cowen suggests a reframe, take it; the grant often follows the reframe.** She confirms grants are "tens of thousands of US dollars, not millions," paperwork under a week, money wired within another week, reporting a single annual one-pager.

*The Kool Aid Factory* applicant modeled a clean budget ask: "$50k: $25k for the designer... and $25k [for a co-founder]." This applicant was first rejected (for a different EV fellowship), and Cowen personally suggested reapplying as a business. **Lesson: name a specific figure, break it into 2-3 concrete line items, and reapply after reframing if rejected.**

*Amos Wollen* (three-time grantee) confirms the application is "very short — 1,500 words," that most applications are rejected, that a lucky subset gets an unpredictable Zoom call ("he'll ask you whatever he feels like asking"), and that there is "no observable pattern among the projects Tyler decides to fund." He points all applicants to Sam Atis's EV FAQ as the canonical guide.

*Merge Club*'s aggregated grantee tips: "Make sure you have a solid understanding of: What you're building / Why you're building it / What you'll spend the funds on." Response typically within 2-3 weeks; follow up on Twitter/X if you hear nothing; a short 10-15 minute interview may follow.

### On the "consensus view" trick question

This prompt is designed to screen *out* reflexive contrarians. The successful pattern (Warny) is to pick a sincerely held mainstream view and defend it rigorously with evidence — ideally one that connects to your project's thesis. The trap is trying to be edgy or clever.

## Recommendations

**Stage 1 — Framing the project (do this before writing).**
- **Lead with the person, not the book.** EV is a talent bet. Your one-line tweet and opening should establish that you are an unusually well-positioned, proven builder in exactly this niche: "I've spent 6+ years building the most-read visual explainers on swarm intelligence; universities already teach from my blog; I've built real drone-swarm autonomy at Anduril, NASA, and BBN; now I want to turn that into the definitive interactive undergraduate textbook on swarm robotics."
- **Position it as a moonshot with a clear problem.** The problem: swarm robotics and multi-robot systems are rising in importance (defense, logistics, space, agriculture) but there is no visual, interactive, undergraduate-friendly textbook; existing material is fragmented papers and dense graduate texts. Your thesis: the field is uniquely suited to interactive/animated explanation (emergent behavior is *visual* and *dynamic*), and a "3Blue1Brown-for-swarms" resource would durably lower the barrier to entry for a generation of students and give professors turnkey teaching material.
- **Make it feel feasible.** This is the single biggest lever. Show a concrete plan: a chapter/module outline, which interactive demos you'll build first, a realistic timeline, and evidence you ship (your 6-year track record, HN front page, adoption as course material). Feasibility is what separates funded from "marginal."
- **Make it scalable and address sustainability.** Even though it's an open educational resource, pre-empt Cowen's revenue question: options include a free web version plus paid print/PDF, institutional licensing or professor "course pack" subscriptions, sponsored/endowed hosting, Patreon/grants, or a companion paid course. State clearly that free-and-open maximizes reach (the social good) while a modest revenue layer makes it durable — invoking EV's own line that "a quick path to revenue self-sufficiency is a feature not a bug."

**Stage 2 — Using your track record (the "About you" section).**
- Foreground *demonstrated agency and follow-through*, not titles: 6+ years and 40k+ views on jasonfantl.com; multiple Hacker News front-page hits; your blog adopted as university course material (this is the killer signal — you have already, informally, done exactly what you're proposing, at smaller scale, and academia validated it).
- Then use the industry work as evidence of *technical depth and credibility*, briefly: Raytheon BBN (networking publications, pending patent), NASA Glenn (delay-tolerant networking, hardware in orbit), Anduril (real drone-swarm autonomy). The Anduril departure over autonomous-weapons concerns can be stated plainly and briefly as evidence of values/authenticity ("some level of caring" is literally one of Cowen's criteria) — but keep it short and non-preachy.
- Mention the Goodfire AI-safety fellowship and the fall-2027 swarm-robotics PhD as evidence of trajectory and seriousness. Frame the textbook as complementary to (not competing with) the PhD: you'll be immersed in the field and can build the resource as you learn and teach.

**Stage 3 — The ask and budget.**
- Name a specific number. Given the norms (median historically $10,000–$25,000), a request in the **$15,000–$50,000** range is credible for a substantial content project; a smaller **$5,000–$15,000** "seed" ask (with an explicit note that you'd welcome follow-on support as you show progress — which Cowen does provide) is the lowest-friction path to a yes.
- Break it into 2-3 concrete line items, e.g.: interactive/animation development tooling and a part-time front-end/animation collaborator; your own time to write and build the first N modules; hosting/domain and open-licensing costs. Keep it to a few lines.
- Optionally offer a "travel grant" framing if useful (e.g., to visit a swarm robotics lab or attend a key conference) — but the core project grant is the stronger play here.

**Stage 4 — Signaling ambition and follow-through.**
- State the ambition explicitly and specifically: not "write a textbook" but "become the default first exposure to swarm robotics for undergraduates worldwide, used in X courses within Y years, with every concept backed by an interactive simulation students can manipulate." Cowen rewards big, articulable visions.
- Prove follow-through with your existing artifacts: link the blog, the specific HN threads, and the course that adopted your material as your multimedia URL / supplementary files (remember: images/docs, not PDF). Let the work speak — it is your best reference letter, and EV takes none.
- For the consensus-view question, give a sincere, evidence-backed mainstream view that ties to the project — for example, that visual and interactive learning materially improves comprehension and retention in technical subjects, or that broadening access to high-quality STEM education has large positive externalities — and cite something concrete.

**Benchmarks that would change this advice.**
- If you can get a warm introduction (a current EV grantee vouching for you, or genuine prior correspondence with Cowen), prioritize that over a cold application — multiple grantees came in through social referral or by emailing Cowen substantive ideas.
- If you are rejected, do not treat it as final: reframe (often toward a more business-like or more clearly scalable version) and reapply — this is a documented, repeated path to a yes.
- If your primary goal shifts from "fund the book" to "get career support and network access," note that "general career support" is itself a common EV grant type, and you could apply on that basis with the textbook as the flagship project.

## Caveats
- **Official vs. grantee speculation.** The three proposal prompts, the ≤1,500-word limit, the no-PDF rule, the "no typical grant range" language, and the ~1-week response target are official (from the Mercatus application form and Cowen's own writing). Dollar figures — the $1,000–$50,000 span, and the historically $10,000–$25,000 median (Chronicle of Philanthropy) — come from a mix of Cowen interviews and third-party grant aggregators (Merge Club, Granted AI, Questd); treat the exact bounds as approximate, since Mercatus deliberately states there is no fixed range. Process details like "10-15 minute interview" and "follow up on Twitter after 2-3 weeks" come from grantee/aggregator accounts, not official guidance.
- **No observable funding pattern.** Multiple grantees stress there is no reliable formula for what Cowen funds; the advice here reflects *revealed patterns and stated criteria*, not a guaranteed rubric. A single evaluator making fast, taste-driven decisions introduces real idiosyncrasy.
- **The canonical grantee FAQ (Sam Atis) could not be read in full** for this report because the source blocks automated access; its existence and role as the most-recommended guide are well corroborated, and you should read it directly before applying (samstack.io / "Emergent Ventures FAQ"). Sample winning applications are also linked (as Google Docs) via Merge Club — including Atis's own writer-focused application, an especially relevant precedent for a content project.
- **EV's funding sources** include the Thiel Foundation, Schmidt Futures, and (historically) Charles Koch's involvement on the Mercatus board; this is not ideological in practice (grantees span the political spectrum), but you should be comfortable with the affiliation.
- **Currency of cohort numbers and figures** reflects sources available as of July 2026; specific grant amounts for named individuals (e.g., the self-reported $7,000 Aceflow grant) are grantee-reported, not confirmed by Mercatus.
