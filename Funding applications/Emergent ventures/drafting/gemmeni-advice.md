A comprehensive report on what you need to know about applying to **Emergent Ventures (EV)**, how Tyler Cowen evaluates proposals, and how to structure a successful application.

---

## Executive Summary: What Makes EV Unique?

Emergent Ventures (administered by the Mercatus Center at George Mason University) is **not** a traditional grantmaking committee. It is essentially a high-throughput venture fund for human agency, run primarily on Tyler Cowen’s judgment.

* **The Primary Evaluator:** Tyler Cowen personally reviews applications, often with help from his network.
* **Zero Bureaucracy:** There are no peer-review committees, multi-round presentations, or heavy administrative hurdles.
* **The Core Question:** *"Will giving this person a grant meaningfully change the trajectory of their project or life?"*
* **Speed:** Decisions are made quickly (often within weeks), and the application is intentionally short (under 1,500 words).

---

## 1. What a Successful Candidate Looks Like

EV funds a wide spectrum of recipients—from 18-year-old self-taught coders to established authors, academic dissidents, and independent researchers. They do not care about traditional credentials, institutional affiliations, or formal degrees.

Winning candidates almost always demonstrate four distinct traits:

1. **Extreme Personal Agency:** You aren't waiting for a publisher, university, or boss to give you permission. You have already started writing, researching, or building.
2. **High-Leverage Thinking:** Your idea isn't just "a book about topic X." It is an intellectual intervention designed to shift public discourse, solve an neglected problem, or create a public good.
3. **Trait Ambition:** You think big, move fast, and aim to be an authority or builder in your domain.
4. **Writing Efficiency:** Your writing is crisp, clear, and direct. EV values concise thought over academic jargon.

## 2. Anatomy of the Application & How to Answer

The application consists of a few concise prompts limited to **1,500 words total**. Here is how to tackle each section effectively:

### Section A: Who are you? (Background & Agency)

* **Goal:** Prove drive and execution capacity.
* **What works:** Focus on what you have *already built or written*, your specific obsession with the topic, and your speed of execution. Keep bio details functional.
* **What fails:** Listing academic honors or credentials without showing recent output.

### Section B: The "Consensus" Question

> *"What is one mainstream or consensus view that you strongly AGREE with?"*

* **Goal:** Test for intellectual sanity, groundedness, and nuance.
* **Why EV asks this:** Cowen uses this to filter out contrarians who are contrarian simply for the sake of it, or people detached from empirical reality.
* **What works:** Pick a core, foundational truth (e.g., in economics, science, or human behavior) that most people accept, and explain briefly *why* it holds true despite common edge-case criticisms.
* **What fails:** Trying to be clever with a fake "consensus" view or answering with an edgy/provocative opinion instead.

### Section C: The Project Proposal (Your Book)

* **Goal:** Articulate the core thesis and its public/intellectual impact.
* **What works:**
* **The Thesis:** In 2–3 sentences, what is the core idea of the book? Why is current thinking on this topic flawed or incomplete?
* **The Distribution Plan:** Writing the book is only 50% of the job. How will people actually read it? (Blog posts, open-access, interactive web tools, newsletter audience, podcast appearances).
* **Public Good Angle:** Why should non-profit grant money support this instead of a commercial publisher advance? (e.g., "Making the research open-source," "Addressing a neglected niche," "Creating educational tools alongside the text").


* **What fails:** Vague descriptions like *"I want to write a book exploring the intersection of technology and society."* Be hyper-specific.

### Section D: Funding & Trajectory

* **Goal:** Justify the budget and demonstrate impact.
* **What works:** State a clear number (e.g., $10,000 to buy out 3 months of dev/research time, cover archival travel, or fund illustrations/visualizations). Show how this grant acts as a catalyst rather than a permanent subsidy.
* **What fails:** Unrealistic budgets without clear allocation, or asking for funding without explaining what gets accomplished faster as a result.

## 3. Specific Guidance for Book & Writing Projects

Tyler Cowen is a prolific reader and author. When evaluating book projects, he is looking for specific indicators:

* **Focus on "Progress Studies" & Novel Paradigms:** Projects exploring decentralized systems, scientific discovery, historical deep-dives into innovation, economic growth, or civilizational resilience score very well.
* **Interactive / Modern Format:** If your book includes public-facing digital artifacts (interactive visualizations, open-source code/simulations, accompanying essays), emphasize this. EV loves media that transcends traditional paper publishing.
* **Speed of Delivery:** Mentioning a tight timeline (e.g., "Draft complete in 6 months, serialized online weekly") demonstrates momentum.

## 4. Checklist Before You Submit

| Check | Requirement |
| --- | --- |
| ☐ | **Under 1,500 words:** Did you keep the overall application tight and readable? |
| ☐ | **Show, Don't Tell:** Did you link to existing drafts, blog posts, or past projects? |
| ☐ | **Clear Public Benefit:** Is it clear why this project creates value beyond personal royalty income? |
| ☐ | **Specific Budget:** Did you request a clear amount tied to tangible milestones? |
| ☐ | **Grounded Consensus Answer:** Is your consensus view answer sensible and well-reasoned? |

---

> **Post-Application Note:** If accepted, EV grants come with minimal reporting requirements (usually a brief update 12 months later) and access to the broader EV grantee network, which is often as valuable as the grant money itself.
