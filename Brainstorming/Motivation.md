The goal is to have an introductory text that an undergraduate could read and fall in love with the field. We want to show how beautiful the work is and how inspiring the future can look.

Many of the existing textbooks, such as *Swarm Robotics: A Formal Approach*, are very detailed, but not inspiring. The field is highly visual and awe-inspiring, so why do our texts not reflect this? Perhaps we need to utilize more animations, as emergent systems are usually only visible over time and as we zoom out.

We see videos on Educational YouTube which are inspiring and popular for niche topics, so clearly it is possible. Although YouTube focuses more on entertainment, its educational value is usually not as strong. We see textbooks struggling to inspire, and we see YouTube videos struggling to teach. Is there a way to draw from each area to help the other?

I think it is possible to create an introductory textbook that is both inspiring and pedagogical, combining the strengths of YouTube and traditional textbooks. However, walking that line between entertainment and pedagogy is something I have seen few people do successfully. The best examples I can think of are Richard Feynman, Carl Sagan, and Grant Sanderson. These are people I would consider exceptional for their skill. It is not an easy thing to come by, but it is clearly possible.

I do not think we need YouTube videos for the field (though that would help). I would prefer to show that the content can itself be entertaining and inspiring. A textbook is much better at teaching than a video (self-paced, rereadable, active instead of passive), so we focus on textbooks, but then utilize the modern medium of screens to elevate the work with animations.

I would like to help readers see what researchers see in their mind's eye. I have spoken to a number of mathematicians, and they say that they can see their work in their minds, sometimes visually, sometimes more abstractly, but that it is beautiful and they wish others could see it too. We have done a poor job as an educational institution showing students this beauty. We teach students topics and see if they find the inspiration on their own. We can do much better. More effort should be spent on helping people feel excited about topics. We focus too much on learning the content and forget that our great discoveries came from people who had fallen in love with their field, and that this love was key.

I see in my mind a self-healing robot swarm, usually on a sprawling farm. Thousands of robots of many different form factors are monitoring and tending to different plants, the trees and garden beds, the seedlings in greenhouses, tractors in the fields, and a repair shop for robots that get damaged. It is an entirely self-sufficient system adapting to changes in weather, the tasks of the day, the churn of robots being available or damaged, and the addition of new robots as the farm expands or changes.

I imagine the communication network as a mesh that needs to manage the flow of information from robots to their neighbors, sometimes to a central node, and sometimes to a partner robot far away. I see the same algorithms adapting from a group of three robots to the sprawling landscape of an entire city, country, or even planet. How might these systems heal? How might they fail? How can they scale, and do they collapse at different sizes? Would systems interact in unexpected ways, and what might we be capable of building?

Being able to work on a protocol and see it scale in your head, to see the implications for the future, to see robots building cities on other planets, exploring deep caverns, putting out devastating wildfires, or finding a missing hiker in a national forest, we need to be able to hope for the future through the work we do.

We can show both the beauty of existing research, like how mathematicians see their work in their minds, and then we can amplify that inspiration with visions of the future.
